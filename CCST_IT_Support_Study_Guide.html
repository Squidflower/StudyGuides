D1IT Support Job Tasks and Responsibilities

IT Support Job Tasks and Responsibilities

The workflow of the help desk: how tickets are prioritized and tracked, how work is measured, and how to document an interaction so it helps the next technician.

1.1Queue and time management

Support work arrives faster than any one person can handle it, so technicians manage a shared queue and their own time deliberately.

Triage & prioritization

Incoming tickets are triaged — assessed and ranked by urgency and impact — rather than handled first-come-first-served. A single VIP outage or a business-wide problem jumps ahead of a routine password reset.

Queue levels & escalation

Support is tiered: Tier 1 handles common issues, escalating to Tier 2/3 when a problem exceeds its scope or time budget. Knowing when to escalate is itself a tested skill — escalate rather than sit on a ticket you can't resolve.

Time management

Technicians balance many open tickets, so they batch similar work, set expectations with users, and avoid over-investing in one ticket at the expense of the queue.

1.2SLAs, KPIs, and ticketing systems

Service-Level Agreement (SLA)

A formal commitment defining response and resolution targets — e.g. "respond within 1 hour, resolve within 8." SLAs set the clock that prioritization must respect.

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

Metrics that measure support quality: first-contact resolution rate, average handle time, ticket volume, and customer satisfaction (CSAT). KPIs show whether SLAs are being met.

Ticketing systems

The system of record for every request — it tracks status, ownership, history, and priority, and is where documentation lives so work is auditable and transferable between technicians.

1.3Documentation

Good documentation is a core deliverable, not an afterthought — it must be useful to someone who wasn't there.

What good documentation looks like

Clear, concise, factual, and complete: describe the problem, the troubleshooting steps you took, and the results. Write it so a future technician (or the same user later) can understand and reuse it. Capture key system facts — hostname, hardware (CPU, memory, disk), OS version, IPv4/IPv6 address, MAC address — when relevant.

Watch for: scenario questions where the best answer is to escalate or to document thoroughly rather than keep troubleshooting — the exam rewards proper process, not heroics.
D2Hardware Issues

Hardware Issues

Identifying components, the ports that connect them, safe handling, and diagnosing common failures in desktops, laptops, printers, and mobile devices.

2.1Safety

Protect yourself and the equipment

ESD (electrostatic discharge) can silently destroy components, so use an anti-static wrist strap and mat and work on a grounded surface. Guard against electrical shock (unplug before opening a power supply; never open a PSU or CRT). Follow fire safety (use a Class C extinguisher for electrical fires) and general personal safety (lifting, cable management).

2.2Ports and interfaces

Video ports

HDMI and DisplayPort carry digital video and audio (modern standard); DVI is digital video only; VGA is legacy analog. USB-C can also drive displays (DisplayPort/Thunderbolt alt-mode).

USB and connectivity

USB comes in versions (2.0, 3.x) and form factors (Type-A, Type-B, Micro, USB-C, which is reversible and increasingly universal for data, video, and power). Ethernet (RJ-45) ports provide wired network access.

Adapters vs. converters

An adapter changes the connector's shape between compatible signals (e.g. USB-C to USB-A); a converter actively changes the signal type (e.g. HDMI to VGA, which converts digital to analog).

2.3Components and upgrades

Core components

CPU (processor) executes instructions; motherboard ties everything together; RAM is fast volatile working memory; storage (HDD spinning disk vs. faster SSD/NVMe) holds data persistently; PSU supplies power; peripherals attach externally.

Installing and upgrading

Match the form factor (physical size/standard) and check compatibility (e.g. CPU socket, RAM type/speed) before installing. Follow a post-installation checklist: reseat, power on, confirm the component is recognized in BIOS/UEFI and the OS.

BIOS/UEFI and POST

BIOS/UEFI is the firmware that initializes hardware at boot; POST (power-on self-test) checks components first — POST beep codes or on-screen errors point to which part failed (e.g. no RAM detected).

2.4Diagnosing hardware failures

Common symptoms

No power (check PSU/outlet), no display (check cable/monitor/GPU), overheating and shutdowns (dust/failed fan), random crashes or memory errors (bad RAM), clicking/slow drive or SMART errors (failing HDD), swollen laptop battery (replace immediately).

Printers & peripherals

Help users with a multifunction device: confirm connectivity, load paper, clear a paper jam, clear the print queue when jobs are stuck, and swap toner/ink. For peripherals, verify cabling, power, and drivers first.

D3Connectivity and Resource Access Issues

Connectivity and Resource Access Issues

Getting users onto the network and to the resources they need — wired and wireless access, IP basics, and the commands that reveal where connectivity breaks.

3.1Network access

Wired vs. wireless

LAN (wired) access via an Ethernet cable is faster and more reliable; WLAN (Wi-Fi) is convenient but affected by range, interference, and congestion. Choose or troubleshoot based on the user's need.

How a device gets on the network

Most hosts use DHCP to automatically receive an IP address, subnet mask, default gateway, and DNS server. If DHCP fails, the host self-assigns an APIPA address (169.254.x.x) and can only reach the local link.

3.2IP addressing basics

The four settings that matter

To reach beyond the local network a host needs a correct IP address and subnet mask (its identity/segment), a default gateway (the router to leave the subnet), and a DNS server (to turn names into IPs). A wrong value in any one produces a distinct symptom.

Private vs. public & IPv4/IPv6

Private addresses (10.x, 172.16–31.x, 192.168.x) are used inside and translated by NAT to a public address for the internet. Hosts increasingly have both an IPv4 and an IPv6 address; you should be able to locate both plus the MAC address on a device.

3.3Diagnosing connectivity

A small set of commands isolates almost every everyday connectivity problem.

The essential commands

ipconfig (Windows) / ifconfig or ip (macOS/Linux) show the host's IP, mask, gateway, and DNS. ping tests reachability. tracert/traceroute shows the path and where it fails. nslookup confirms DNS resolution.

Reading the symptom

169.254.x.x → DHCP failure. Can ping by IP but not by name → DNS problem. Local hosts reachable but internet isn't → wrong default gateway/route. Nothing works and no link light → physical (cable/port). This symptom-to-cause mapping is heavily tested.

Watch for: "check the DNS server" versus "check the default gateway" — the giveaway is whether name resolution or off-network reachability is what's broken.
D4Operating System and Application Issues

Operating System and Application Issues

Supporting Windows, macOS, and mobile platforms: configuration, user data, updates, and fixing misbehaving applications.

4.1Windows support

Key tools

Task Manager (end frozen apps, watch CPU/memory), Control Panel / Settings (configuration), Device Manager (drivers/hardware), Event Viewer (logs), Command Prompt/PowerShell, and Disk Management. You should know where a user finds their IP (ipconfig or Settings > Network).

Accounts & updates

Understand standard vs. administrator accounts, user profiles, and Windows Update for patches. Many issues resolve with a restart, a driver update, or a pending update being installed.

4.2macOS support

macOS essentials

Finder (files), System Settings (configuration), Activity Monitor (the macOS Task Manager), Disk Utility, and Force Quit for stuck apps. Software comes via the App Store and system updates.

Backup & restore

Help users protect data with Apple's tools: Time Machine for local/networked full backups and iCloud for cloud sync and restore of personal data across devices.

4.3Mobile device support

Common mobile tasks

Restart a frozen phone; troubleshoot a device that won't charge (cable, port debris, adapter); fix Wi-Fi/cellular connectivity; set up email and mobile apps; and support collaboration software.

MDM

A basic understanding of Mobile Device Management — the platform that lets an organization enforce policy, push apps/config, and remotely wipe lost or stolen devices.

4.4Application issues

Fixing misbehaving apps

Standard remedies in order: close and reopen, check for updates, verify compatibility and system requirements, confirm the user has permissions/licensing, clear cache, then repair or reinstall. Confirm the problem is the app and not connectivity or the OS.

D5Common Threats and Preventions

Common Threats and Preventions

Recognizing the threats end users face, doing a basic investigation, escalating correctly, and understanding the policies that protect user data.

5.1Security threats to the end user

The threats you must recognize

Phishing — fraudulent messages that trick users into revealing credentials or clicking malicious links (spear phishing is targeted). Malware — viruses, worms, ransomware, spyware. Spam — bulk unwanted mail, often a delivery vehicle. Unauthorized access attempts — someone trying to log in or reach data they shouldn't. Spoofing — faking a trusted identity (email, caller ID, website). Impersonation — a social-engineering ploy pretending to be IT, a manager, or a vendor.

Basic investigation & escalation

As frontline support you perform a basic investigation (inspect the suspicious email header/link, note symptoms, check for spread) and then escalate to the appropriate team (security/incident response) — you contain and report, you don't attempt deep remediation beyond your role.

5.2Prevention and mitigation

Everyday defenses

Keep systems patched, run antimalware, use strong passwords and MFA, apply least privilege, be cautious with email links/attachments, and educate users. Prompt reporting of a suspected phishing message is a key preventive behavior.

5.3Policies and confidentiality

Protecting user data

Company policies and confidentiality guidelines govern how data is handled. Learn to identify confidential, proprietary, and personally identifiable information (PII) — and treat each according to policy. Support staff must respect privacy, verify identity before sharing information, and never expose PII.

Watch for: the right first move on a suspected phishing/security event is usually to report/escalate — not to click, forward, or try to "fix" it yourself.
D6Job Tools (The IT Professional)

Job Tools

The tools and professional practices that make support effective: remote access, collaboration, research, and the soft skills the exam scenarios assume.

6.1Remote access tools

Supporting users remotely

Remote access software lets you connect to an end user's device to see the screen and perform support tasks. Best practices: get the user's consent, confirm you're on the right machine, and use secure, sanctioned tools — never expose credentials during a session.

6.2Collaboration tools

Working with the team and users

Video conferencing and persistent chat (e.g. Cisco Webex) keep support and users connected, allow screen sharing, and preserve conversation history for follow-up and documentation.

6.3Research tools

Finding answers

Resolve unfamiliar issues using a knowledge base (internal documented solutions — check here first), technical forums (community experience), and search engines. Evaluate sources critically before applying a fix.

Artificial intelligence

Predictive AI anticipates issues from patterns; generative AI drafts responses and summarizes. Use AI as an aid, but verify its output and mind privacy, security, and ethics — never paste confidential data or PII into an unsanctioned tool.

6.4Professionalism

Communication that the exam assumes

Although not always tested as a discrete item, scenarios expect: professional communication, active listening, explaining technical concepts to non-technical users in plain language, patience and empathy, and matching your vocabulary to the audience. The "best" answer usually reflects good service conduct, not just the technically correct fix.

Exam strategy

  • 50 minutes for ~40–50 questions — the exam is scenario-based, so read for what a good technician would actually do, not just the textbook fact.
  • When two answers are technically valid, the better one usually respects process: escalate appropriately, document thoroughly, get user consent, protect PII.
  • Drill the connectivity symptom → cause table (169.254 = DHCP, name-only failure = DNS, off-network failure = gateway) — it shows up across Domains 3 and 4.
  • Know ports/interfaces by sight (HDMI vs. DisplayPort vs. VGA, USB-C) and safe-handling basics (ESD, PSU).
